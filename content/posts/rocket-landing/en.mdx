## Why it falls on its belly

A Starship is a 50.3 m tube, 9 m across. Nose-first it shows 64 m² to the air; lying flat it shows 450 m², seven
times as much.

Seven times the area is almost three times the terminal velocity: **66 m/s on its belly against 177 m/s nose-first**.
So the whole descent is a skydive, and the four flaps are arms and legs — which also means they only work while the
air is moving, and lose their grip exactly when it slows down.

## How many engines to light is a real decision

The ship carries six engines: three sea-level Raptors and three vacuum ones. (The 33 everyone remembers belong to
the booster.) Only the sea-level three matter for landing, and **how many of them to light is the decision the whole
manoeuvre turns on**.

One Raptor gives 2,256 kN and throttles down to 40 %, so a single engine covers 0.90–2.26 MN. The ship weighs about
128 t at touchdown, which is 1.26 MN:

| lit | thrust range | against its own weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.90–2.26 MN | its weight **falls inside the range** — it can ask for exactly what it needs |
| 2 | 1.80–4.51 MN | the floor is already 1.4× the weight |
| 3 | 2.71–6.77 MN | the floor is 2.2× the weight |

One engine straddles the weight; two cannot. On two or more, lighting them means accelerating upward and cutting
them means falling — all it can do is stutter down. On one, it can hold any descent rate it likes, like a throttle.

So the autopilot in figure 1 never decides on a number of engines. It works out **the thrust it wants, then lights
the fewest engines that can produce it**. In a single descent that changes three times:

| height | falling at | it lights |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1434 m | 69 m/s | nothing — belly-down |
| 770 m | 67 m/s | **1** × 53 %, and the flip begins |
| 105 m | 41 m/s | **2** × 50 %, because one cannot brake this |
| 42 m | 5 m/s | **1** × 90 % for the last forty metres |

Nobody wrote that sequence. I gave it one equation — how much thrust do you want — and the rest is arithmetic. Across
eight independent training runs, **every single one chose to flip on one engine**, and to brake on two, which is what
the real ship does.

## The flip throws the ship sideways

This is the thing the simulator taught me, and it explains why the real flight path looks the way it does.

When the engines light, the ship is still horizontal, so they are pointing sideways. Measured: **the flip itself
moves the ship 64 m across and leaves it with 21.5 m/s of sideways speed** — the remaining hundred-odd metres are
covered by coasting on that.

So it cannot aim at the target. The real answer is to **come down deliberately off to one side** and let the flip
throw you onto the pad. In figure 1 the ship always starts 150–270 m to the left, 207 m on average.

My first version dropped it straight at the pad, and no amount of training got above 0 % — the manoeuvre itself
carried it away. Offset to one side, the same trainer reached 200 of 200 in ten rounds. The real trajectory is
shaped that way for a reason.

## Letting it find the ten numbers

The shape of the manoeuvre is fixed, and it is SpaceX's: fall flat, hold the belly into the airflow with the flaps,
light the engines at some height, swing upright, and arrive with nothing left over. **What is learned is ten
constants** — the ignition height, how hard to swing, how much braking margin to keep, the touchdown speed to aim
for, how stiffly to hold the attitude, how much to correct sideways, the flip throttle, how many engines for the
flip, the most to allow while braking, and how hard to chase the target speed.

The trainer is the cross-entropy method, about as simple as search gets: draw 40 sets of numbers, fly 10 descents
with each, keep the best 6, and redraw from their mean and spread. No gradients, no network, 35 rounds and done.

That is what runs when you press "train one" in figure 1 — in your browser, in about a second. Ten different random
seeds, each flown 200 unseen descents: **nine of the ten land more than 80 % of the time, with a median of 95 %**.

The tenth never lands at all. The difference is one choice: **those nine flip on a single engine and it flips on
two.** Twice the thrust while the ship is still sideways throws it too far off the pad to recover.

Which is also why the search must not settle too quickly. CEM draws each round from the spread of the previous
round's elite, and the elite agree on "two, then" very easily; adding a little extra noise that fades out over the
run moved the median from 88 % to 95 %.

## Handing it to a neural network

Those ten numbers are a shape I chose; the trainer only fills in the blanks. So figure 1 has a second button: teach
a neural network — 8 × 24 × 3, a little over seven hundred weights, which knows nothing about landing. State in,
controls out.

First it **copies** (behaviour cloning): the autopilot flies 120 descents, every state and action is written down,
and the network fits them. It copies well and lands 0 % of the time. What it cannot copy is the part that is not
there: the student's first small error takes the ship somewhere the teacher never went, and there it has nothing.

Then **DAgger**: the *student* flies while the teacher labels every state the student reaches, and those go into the
pile. That is the part that is actually teaching.

And then it learned to hover. It copied beautifully from 1450 m down to about 40 m and then stopped — hanging
between 3 and 16 m for ninety seconds until the clock ran out. Not a crash. It simply never landed. The reason is
the thrust table above: when firing is a switch, a duty cycle a few percent too generous balances gravity exactly.

The fix was not a parameter. It was **the question**. The network had three heads — fire or not, how hard, how many
engines — three discrete decisions that all have to agree. Replacing them with **one number, the thrust it wants**
(in units of the ship's own weight), fed through the same arithmetic the autopilot uses, with the same training and
the same seconds:

| seed | 7 | 19 | 31 | 43 | 57 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| three heads | 0 % | 0 % | 0 % | 0 % | 0 % |
| one thrust number | 46.5 % | 4.5 % | **98.0 %** | 88.5 % | 60.0 % |

The best of them matches the teacher: 98.0 % at 1.01 m/s. The worst is useless.

Which is the thing worth taking away: **ten numbers land every time you train them; seven hundred weights copying
those ten numbers land anywhere between 5 % and 98 %, depending on where they started.** More parameters is not more
learning, and how you ask matters more than how big it is.

## When the reward is wrong, it does exactly what you wrote

I wrote the score, so the mistakes in it are mine too. Same simulator, same trainer, three ways of writing it:

| the reward I wrote | what it became |
| --- | --- |
| 500 for landing, plus up to 60 for leftover fuel (**only if it landed**) | 97.5 % landed, 1.2 m/s, 14.7 t left |
| the same, but fuel counts **whether it lands or not** | 96.0 % landed — the trap did not spring |
| "stay alive, and be near the pad" | **15 % landed**, engines lit 86 % of the time, 106 of 200 ran the tanks dry and 54 were still in the air at the 90-second cutoff |

The third row is reward hacking out of the textbook. I wanted "don't crash" and I wrote "stay alive," so it learned
to **hover until the propellant ran out** — every step in the air scored, and landing only stopped the scoring. It
was not cheating. It was doing very carefully what I asked.

The second row is the better lesson. I predicted it would learn never to light the engines at all, to keep a full
tank, and I was wrong: the 500 for landing still dominates. Guessing how a reward will break is no more reliable
than guessing what a model will learn, which is why all three rows were measured rather than reasoned.
